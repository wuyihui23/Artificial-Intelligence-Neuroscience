What to try

Counterphase flicker is the sum of a left- and a right-moving grating. Both models give a mean of zero, though single detectors still fluctuate.

Reverse-phi dots jump rightward while their contrast inverts at every step. People see them move leftward, flies turn leftward, and both models also signal leftward. That is strong evidence that real motion detection multiplies (correlates) signals.

Moving bar vs grating: the Reichardt output for a grating is steady, while for a bar or dots it comes in bursts, because the correlation is only nonzero when an edge passes.

Slow the delay τ and the Reichardt band moves to lower temporal frequencies. Fly T4/T5 cells peak at about 1–3 Hz, so τ is roughly 50–150 ms.

The two models in equations

Reichardt (grating, contrast c, spatial freq f, temporal freq ω): R = c² · sin(2π f Δx) · ωτ / (1 + ω²τ²) peak at ω = 1/τ (temporal-frequency tuning) Motion energy: E_dir = (g_even,dir ∗ s)² + (g_odd,dir ∗ s)² opponent = E_right − E_left

Both are a linear stage followed by a multiplication or squaring. For gratings their opponent outputs are mathematically equivalent (van Santen & Sperling 1985). They differ in filter shapes, so their tuning maps look different here.

Simulation: 1D strip of 32° sampled every 0.25°, time step 2 ms. Reichardt arms use a first-order low-pass delay; motion-energy filters are space–time Gabors (σx = 0.6/f0, σt = 60 ms, centred 120 ms in the past). Values are in model units, normalized so a full-contrast optimal grating gives 1. Real T4/T5 cells combine Reichardt-like enhancement with Barlow–Levick null-direction suppression (Haag et al. 2016).
